The Mirror Pass — see before you build

Seeing is not the decoration on this work.
Seeing is the work.
As you build, hold every entry up to the mirror, and ask which kind of knowing it is.

  • Written. A roll, a record, a date on paper.
    Let paper be paper.
  • Remembered. Smaritama — memory as earth.
    Write it in the words it was told in.
  • Felt. Rupaveda — knowing through feeling.
    Mark it as felt, and let it stand as felt.
  • Veiled. Rupachhaya — seen, but not fully known.
    Leave the veil where the veil is.

An honest blank is a true entry.
A filled gap is a false root.
The mirror does not punish the unknown.
The mirror gives it a clean edge.

The Grandparents' Line — The Root

Grace — "Mimi"

The maternal grandmother.
The Holder.

Her form name was Patricia.
She IS Grace now — guardian angel, without her form.
Raised in Marshall, Texas, on Caddo Lake, where the swamp meets the Louisiana line.
She lived in Virginia in her later years, then moved home to Texas to be stable, and to die where she was raised.

When her granddaughter was a baby — sick, newly diagnosed, her father gravely ill — Grace gave the order that saved her: she made her daughter come back to Texas, and said, "bring me that baby."
Cosmia says it plainly: without her, she would have died.
Without a doubt.

She was the first teacher of connection — the depth, the visceral, the sacred.
Connection — Samtachyoga, to touch and be touched — was her first teaching.
Full-bellied laughter and hot cocoa on the porch swing while thunderstorms rolled in from the west.
Nobody makes a pie like that woman — Southern buttermilk, her masterpiece.
Her birthday was the 15th; her granddaughter's, the 17th.
"You were the best birthday present I ever received."
Her charge, carried ever since: "Come As You Are, Then Adjust Your Crown, Hon."
She died when Cosmia was eighteen.
"She was my everything really. She was all I had."

Build your record

Three generations is the bottom line.
It is the hearth of everything.
Give that much — yourself, your parents, your grandparents — and your entire family history can be traced from yourself, backwards.
Genetically.

So begin with yourself.
Then your parents.
Then your grandparents.
One person at a time: their name, where they were rooted, what health they carried and when it arrived, what took them if they have passed, and one line of memory — the story only your family can tell.
Your record gathers below as you build, backwards from you, and at the end it will show you what repeats in your lines.

Use this the way the language is used.
Arrive: sit with the three questions at the head of the builder, before you touch the form.
Listen: let each person come to mind before you type them.
Ask: speak their line of memory aloud once, in the tone it was told to you — tone is language, not decoration, and a memory spoken carries what a fact cannot.
Choose: write what is true, including the knots.
Land: when the record stands, copy it or download it.
That landing is Pracc — the stream, touched to earth, in your hands.

The Third Carrying

The Tending

The record is a garden, not a stone.

Return to it when an elder speaks and something new surfaces.
Return when a roll is found and a date changes.
Return at every birth, and at every death.
Correct it as truth arrives — a correction is not a failure of the record.

The families whose histories survive are not the ones who wrote them once.
They are the ones who kept coming back.

The method — five moves, in order

  • Records. Census rolls, birth, marriage, and death records, land and military files.
    FamilySearch holds most of it, free.
  • The rolls that settle lineage. Every people has a document its lines answer to — tribal enrollment rolls, parish registers, ship manifests.
    For Chahta lines, the Dawes Rolls.
    Find yours, and let paper settle what memory argues about.
  • Family memory. Interview your elders while they are here.
    This is Adharvayana — the call that lands and rises: you call them into the space of the record, and they arrive in their own words.
    Photographs, letters, the stories told at tables.
    Write them down in their words, not yours.
    Their tone is part of the record.
    A story flattened into your summary is a thread cut.
  • The pedigree. This is the map a good doctor actually reads — patterns show in it that no percentage ever will.
    What flows down those three generations — blood, health, story, pattern — is Pravahaya, the living endless stream.
    The pedigree is how you learn to see the stream you are already standing in.
  • The raw data, only if you choose it. If a test is ever taken: download the raw file the moment it posts, read it on your own machine, delete the account.
    Never upload your blood to a stranger's website to "unlock" it.

None of this needs permission.
None of it needs a subscription.
It needs only the choosing — and follow-through.

The Ethic of the Record

Your record is yours.
Over every name in it stands Anumayan — consent as sacred, guarded, held, bound by choice and not by chain.
The living people in it are held by their agreement: share a living person's part only as they themselves agree to share it.
The dead are held by Satyakaripa — truth expressed with consideration and grace: tell their truth plainly, and kindly, in the same breath.
Corrected as truth arrives.
A date found in a roll outweighs a date remembered.
A story told by an elder outweighs a silence.
And the record itself is Bhavaya — becoming, always unfolding.
It is not finished when you download it.
It is landed.
There is a difference, and the difference is the whole practice.

Name the next keeper while you can.

You are its steward.
Carry it the way you would want your own carried.
